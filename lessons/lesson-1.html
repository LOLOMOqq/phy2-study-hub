Lesson 1: Electric Charge, Forces & Fields

Made simple: learn the idea in plain words, then use the formulas step by step.

Source: PHY2- D2 2.pdf • Tip: Read the “In plain words” parts first, then open details only if you need more.

What is charge?A property of matter: positive (+) or negative (−). Opposites pull together, same signs push apart.
What is a force?The push or pull between charges. Closer or bigger charges = stronger push/pull.
What is a field?The “invisible influence” around a charge that tells other charges how they would be pushed/pulled.
How to solve problems1) Convert units 2) Draw arrows 3) Use formula 4) Check direction

Quick Review (plain words)

  • Charge basics: Protons are +, electrons are −, neutrons have no charge. Same signs push apart, different signs pull together.
  • How things get charged: Usually electrons move. If an object gains electrons it becomes negative; if it loses electrons it becomes positive.
  • Materials: Conductors (like metals) let electrons move easily, so charge spreads out. Insulators (like rubber/glass) hold electrons tightly, so charge stays where it is.
  • Charge comes in tiny packets: \(q = Ne\). You can think of \(e\) as the charge of 1 electron (very, very small).
  • Coulomb’s law (how strong?): \(F = k\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2}\). More charge = stronger. More distance = weaker (a lot weaker, because of \(r^2\)).
  • Electric field (the influence): \(E = F/q_0\). It tells you the push/pull a +1 C test charge would feel at a point. Field points away from + and toward −.
  • More than one charge: Don’t just add numbers blindly. Find the direction of each push/pull, then combine them (add/subtract on a line, or use components in 2D).

Concepts Explained Simply (open for more help)

A. Atoms & electric charge — what’s actually happening?

In plain words: Everything is made of atoms. Atoms have + parts (protons), − parts (electrons), and neutral parts (neutrons). An object becomes charged when it has more electrons than protons (negative) or fewer electrons than protons (positive).

  • Important: in everyday charging, electrons move. Protons usually stay in the nucleus.
  • Neutral does not mean “no charges”: it means the + and − are balanced.
  • Example idea: if you gain 3 electrons, you become more negative by 3 electron-charges.

B. Conductors vs insulators — why charge behaves differently

In plain words: The difference is how easily electrons can move.

  • Conductor (copper, aluminum, silver): electrons can move around → extra charge spreads out, especially on the surface.
  • Insulator (glass, rubber, wood): electrons cannot move freely → extra charge mostly stays where you put it.
  • Easy comparison: conductor = open hallway, insulator = sticky floor.

C. Charging methods — 3 ways electrons get redistributed

In plain words: Charging means electrons moved or shifted.

  • Friction (rubbing): rub two things → electrons transfer. Example: glass + silk: electrons go to silk (silk becomes −, glass becomes +).
  • Conduction (contact/touch): a charged object touches another object → electrons can move because they are in contact.
  • Induction (no touch): bring a charged object close (but not touching) → electrons inside the nearby object rearrange (one side becomes +, other side becomes −).
  • Quick check: friction/conduction involve transfer; induction starts with rearrangement caused by a nearby charge.

D. Conservation and quantization — the “accounting rules”

  • Conservation of charge: charge is not created or destroyed, it just moves. If one object becomes −, another becomes + by the same amount.
  • Quantization: \(q = Ne\). Charge comes in electron-sized steps, so \(N\) is how many electrons were gained/lost.

E. Coulomb’s law — how strong is the push/pull?

In plain words: Coulomb’s law tells you the strength of the electric force between two charges.

  • Formula: \(F = k\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2}\)
  • What makes it stronger: bigger charges → stronger force.
  • What makes it weaker: bigger distance → weaker force, and it drops fast (\(r^2\) means 2× farther = 4× weaker).
  • Direction: same signs repel, opposite signs attract.
  • Easy method: use the formula to get the number (magnitude), then decide left/right or toward/away using the signs.

F. Electric field — the “influence” a charge creates in space

In plain words: A charge affects the space around it. That effect is called the electric field. If you put another charge there, the field tells it which way and how strongly to move.

  • Definition: \(E = F/q_0\) means “force per 1 coulomb of (positive) test charge.”
  • From a point charge: \(E = k\frac{|q|}{r^2}\) (same distance rule as Coulomb’s law).
  • Direction rule (easy): field goes away from + and toward −.
  • If a negative charge is placed in the field: it feels force in the opposite direction of \(\vec{E}\).
  • Multiple sources: find each field’s direction at the point, then add them as vectors.

G. Gauss’s law — a shortcut for special shapes

In plain words: Gauss’s law helps you find the electric field when the charge is spread out in a very neat/symmetrical way (sphere, long wire, large plate). Instead of adding up many tiny forces, you use the symmetry to get the answer faster.

  • Main equation: \(\Phi_E = \oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}\) (flux through a closed surface = enclosed charge / \(\varepsilon_0\)).
  • Simple idea of flux: how much electric field “passes through” a surface.

Results you need to know (by shape)

  • Uniformly charged sphere:
    • Outside the sphere: acts like a point charge at the center → \(E = k\frac{Q}{r^2}\) (gets weaker with distance).
    • Inside a solid uniformly charged sphere: field grows from 0 at the center to maximum at the surface (\(E \propto r\)).
    • Inside a charged conductor (in electrostatic equilibrium): \(E=0\).
  • Long uniformly charged wire/line: field points straight out from (or in toward) the wire and weakens more slowly: \(E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \frac{2k\lambda}{r}\).
  • Large uniformly charged plate/sheet: field is nearly the same size near the sheet and points perpendicular to it: \(E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\).

Memory tip: sphere → like point charge (\(1/r^2\)), wire → \(1/r\), large sheet → almost constant.

How to Solve Problems (easy step-by-step)
  1. Write what you know: list charges, distances, and what you need to find.
  2. Convert first: change \(\mu\text{C}\to\text{C}\), \(\text{cm}\to\text{m}\), etc. (Most mistakes happen here.)
  3. Draw and mark arrows: decide which way each force/field points before calculating.
  4. Calculate the number: plug into the formula to get magnitude (size).
  5. Combine directions: on a straight line, call one side + and other −. In 2D, split into x/y parts.
  6. Final check: Is the answer’s direction reasonable? Did you square \(r\)? Are units correct?

Quick rule for directions: Force: same repel, opposite attract. Field: away from +, toward −. A negative charge feels force opposite the field.

Worked Examples (with reasoning)

Example 1: Electrons in 1.00 C

Idea: each electron carries charge \(e\), so total charge is \(q=Ne\).
Compute: \(N = q/e = 1.00/(1.60\times10^{-19}) = 6.25\times10^{18}\) electrons.
Meaning: one coulomb is a huge number of electrons because \(e\) is tiny.

Example 2: Excess electrons for \(4.8\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}\)

Compute: \(N = (4.8\times10^{-19})/(1.6\times10^{-19}) = 3\).
Meaning: net charge corresponds to 3 extra electron charges.

Example 3: Coulomb force (attraction)

Given: \(q_1=+4\times10^{-6}\,\text{C}\), \(q_2=-2\times10^{-6}\,\text{C}\), \(r=0.2\,\text{m}\).
Magnitude: \(F = k\frac{|q_1q_2|}{r^2} = (8.99\times10^9)\frac{(4\times10^{-6})(2\times10^{-6})}{(0.2)^2} \approx 1.8\,\text{N}\).
Direction: opposite signs → attractive (each pulls toward the other).

Example 4: Force in a known electric field

Given: \(E=2.0\,\text{N/C}\). Use \(F=q_0E\).
(a) \(q_0=+18.0\times10^{-8}\,\text{C} \Rightarrow F=3.6\times10^{-7}\,\text{N}\) in the same direction as \(\vec{E}\).
(b) \(q_0=-24.0\times10^{-8}\,\text{C} \Rightarrow F=4.8\times10^{-7}\,\text{N}\) in the opposite direction to \(\vec{E}\).
Why: field direction is defined by force on a positive test charge.

Example 5: Electric field of a point charge

Given: \(Q=5.00\,\text{mC}=5.00\times10^{-3}\,\text{C}\), \(r=2.00\,\text{m}\).
Compute: \(E = k\frac{|Q|}{r^2} = (8.99\times10^9)\frac{5.00\times10^{-3}}{(2.00)^2} = 1.12\times10^7\,\text{N/C}\).
Direction: away from \(Q\) if \(Q\) is positive, toward \(Q\) if negative.

Mastery Practice (with hints)

Level 1 — Basics

  1. How many electrons correspond to \(3.20\times10^{-19}\,\text{C}\)? (Use \(N=q/e\))
  2. \(5.0\times10^{12}\) electrons removed from a neutral object: net charge? (Removed electrons → positive object)
  3. Charge spreads quickly over the surface: conductor or insulator?
  4. Electric field due to a positive source charge points toward or away?

Level 2 — Apply formulas

  1. \(+3.0\,\mu\text{C}\) and \(+6.0\,\mu\text{C}\), \(0.30\,\text{m}\) apart: magnitude and nature of force?
  2. If distance triples, force changes by what factor? (Think \(1/r^2\))
  3. Electron in \(E=250\,\text{N/C}\) to the right: force magnitude and direction?
  4. Electric field \(4.0\,\text{cm}\) from \(+20\,\text{nC}\)? (Convert cm → m)

Level 3 — Vectors/superposition

  1. Line: \(q_1=+2.0\,\mu\text{C}\) at \(x=0\), \(q_2=-3.0\,\mu\text{C}\) at \(0.20\,\text{m}\), \(q_3=+4.0\,\mu\text{C}\) at \(0.50\,\text{m}\). Net force on \(q_2\)? (Find pull from left and right, then subtract by direction)
  2. Same setup: net electric field at \(q_2\) due to \(q_1,q_3\)? (\(\vec{E}=\vec{F}/q_2\) or add fields directly)
  3. Equilateral triangle, side \(15.0\,\text{cm}\), \(Q=+7.00\,\mu\text{C}\) each: net force on top charge? (Horizontal components cancel, verticals add)

Final Boss

  1. \(q_0=+2.0\times10^{-9}\,\text{C}\) is \(0.10\,\text{m}\) right of \(Q_1=+5.0\,\mu\text{C}\) and \(0.20\,\text{m}\) left of \(Q_2=-5.0\,\mu\text{C}\). Find (a) \(E\) from \(Q_1\), (b) \(E\) from \(Q_2\), (c) \(E_{net}\), (d) \(F_{net}\). (Check direction of each field at \(q_0\) first)
Answer Key (with brief explanations)
  1. \(N=q/e=2\) electrons.
  2. Removed electrons → \(q=+Ne=+8.0\times10^{-7}\,\text{C}\).
  3. Conductor (mobile charges spread over surface).
  4. Away from a positive source charge.
  5. \(F=k\frac{(3.0e-6)(6.0e-6)}{0.30^2}=1.80\,\text{N}\), repulsive (like charges).
  6. \(1/3^2 = 1/9\) of original force.
  7. \(F=|q|E=4.0\times10^{-17}\,\text{N}\), direction left (electron opposite field).
  8. Convert \(4.0\,\text{cm}=0.040\,\text{m}\): \(E=k\frac{20\times10^{-9}}{0.040^2}=1.12\times10^5\,\text{N/C}\).
  9. On \(q_2\): attraction to \(q_1\) gives \(1.35\,\text{N}\) left; attraction to \(q_3\) gives \(1.20\,\text{N}\) right → net \(0.15\,\text{N}\) left.
  10. \(E_{net}=F_{net}/|q_2|=5.0\times10^4\,\text{N/C}\) right (opposite net force on negative charge; or add \(\vec{E}_1,\vec{E}_3\) directly).
  11. One-corner force \(F_1=19.6\,\text{N}\); horizontal parts cancel, verticals add: \(2F_1\cos30^\circ=33.9\,\text{N}\) upward.
  12. At \(q_0\): \(E_1\) from +\(Q_1\) is right (\(4.495\times10^6\,\text{N/C}\)); \(E_2\) from −\(Q_2\) is also right (\(1.124\times10^6\,\text{N/C}\)); so \(E_{net}=5.62\times10^6\,\text{N/C}\) right and \(F_{net}=q_0E_{net}=1.12\times10^{-2}\,\text{N}\) right.

Common Mistakes (and how to avoid them)

  • Skipping unit conversion: always convert to C and m before using formulas.
  • Forgetting \(r^2\): doubling distance cuts force/field to 1/4, not 1/2.
  • Ignoring direction: draw arrows and use vector sums; magnitudes alone aren’t enough for superposition.
  • Field direction confusion: \(\vec{E}\) shows force direction on a positive test charge; negative charges move opposite \(\vec{E}\).
  • Mixing source and test charge roles: source creates the field; test charge experiences it.
